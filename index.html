The Octopus

Modular water quality monitor · 2024–present

Hurricane Helene rerouted rivers across the Appalachian Mountains and washed sediment and chemical runoff into water supplies, including in the western North Carolina towns where I have family. To measure the damage, agencies had to send people out again and again to collect thousands of samples by hand, pulling time and money from the rest of the recovery.

That gap hits low-income communities hardest. Traditional monitoring equipment is expensive, so the people with the worst water often have the least information about how dangerous it is. I built the Octopus to close that gap. It's a monitor built on an ESP32 and a custom PCB that reads up to eight interchangeable sensors and turns raw numbers into plain-language results on a live web dashboard, so non-experts can use it. The whole build costs about $300 in parts.

Design decisions

The device computes 14+ derived insights on the board itself, so it works with no internet connection. If a sensor fails, the firmware drops it from the analysis instead of reporting bad numbers. Results are graded by severity so someone without a chemistry background can act on them, and a buzzer sounds when the water looks unsafe. I designed the PCB so a non-expert can set it up from the board alone: every sensor port is labeled, each analog sensor has its own calibration button, and the pin map and quick-start steps are printed on the silkscreen. It isn't tied to one power source. So far I've run it on a LiPo pack, AA batteries, a 9V battery, a wall adapter, and a solar-charged battery. The board also accepts 12V, so in places where power is scarce, a scavenged car battery could keep it running.

Testing it

For my first science fair, I ran trials comparing my grandmother's tap water with water from a nearby lake. The pH readings didn't change the way they should have, and because the dashboard shows raw sensor values, I could see the probe was faulty. The same trials showed the ORP probe needed better electrical isolation to measure directly in the lake.

At the next fair, I checked the Octopus against a commercial water testing pen, and the readings lined up closely. For the state fair, I sampled more locations, checked the pH and ORP probes against reference solutions of known value, and tested the flow, water level, and soil moisture sensors. Those early faults are why drift and fault detection are core features of the firmware rewrite I'm doing now in PlatformIO.

Competing with it

I entered the Conrad Challenge with Elizabeth Reed, who handled graphic design, which is a weak point of mine. We built a lean canvas business model, I built the prototype, and our written answers made us one of the top 5 finalists invited to the summit in Houston. There we pitched to judges from water industries and won the Donald James Citizenship Award and the Innovator Award.

Next, Elizabeth and I took it to the K-12 InVenture Prize. Feedback on our mock pitch video showed us what to focus on, and the revised version took 3rd place and the Agriculture & Food award at state. At the Georgia Science & Engineering Fair, the Octopus earned one of the top 10 grand awards among more than 300 senior projects. InVenture also qualified us for Invention Convention U.S. Nationals, where we competed against student inventors from across the country.

Where it's going

Water scientists in Peru and Denver reviewed the design and shaped the next version, including reporting turbidity in NTU. Compassion8Innovation, a Seattle nonprofit, is preparing a stable release for field sampling at local beaches.

ARCS

Autonomous Robot Climbing System · 63rd Governor's Honors Program team build

A robot that drives up a vertical wall, finds cracks with a camera, and fills them with spackle. About 15 of us built it at the Governor's Honors Program, split into teams for the chassis and electrical, thrust, computer vision, movement, and extrusion. It was my first real team engineering project.

How it works

Two electric ducted fans push the robot into the wall hard enough that friction holds it up. Together they make up to 3,155 g of thrust against a 1,677 g robot. A Raspberry Pi camera runs a YOLO segmentation model that finds each crack and sends its coordinates to the ESP32 on board. The robot drives there, and a rack-and-pinion syringe fills the crack.

My part: movement

I was on the movement team, which got the robot to drive on the wall in a controlled way, both by hand and on its own. We picked N20 gear motors light enough to carry and strong enough to climb while the fans pressed them into the wall, with encoders so the robot knew how far it had gone. An H-bridge driver gave us direction, speed, and fast stopping from a separate battery, and a PID loop on the encoder counts took the error out of each move. The last step was getting the robot to drive itself to the coordinates the vision model sent.

What broke

Our Bluetooth manual control lagged badly enough that the robot nearly hit a teammate. I built a replacement handheld controller from an ESP32 and joysticks that talks to the robot's ESP32 directly over ESP-NOW. Latency dropped, and the team switched to it as the default way to drive.

Autonomous driving was close but didn't make it. The encoders failed before the official test, and without them the robot couldn't tell how far it had moved, so it had no way to reach a target coordinate. We ran the official test on my manual controller instead.

FPV claw drone

Georgia TSA Drone Challenge · 9th at state, 2025

A 5-inch FPV drone with a front-mounted claw for picking up light payloads. The challenge required building the drone from scratch, documenting the build, and flying it through obstacles.

Design

The drone runs on a SpeedyBee F405 flight controller. The claw is 3D printed and opened and closed by a small servo, mounted at the front so it reaches the payload before the props do. The servo is wired through the flight controller and mapped to a switch on my radio, so one flick of a lever opens or closes the claw mid-flight. I put the FPV camera directly above the claw so I can see exactly what I'm grabbing from the pilot's view. The battery sits on top of the frame to keep it clear of the claw underneath.

What broke

My first frame was 3D printed for fast iteration. It vibrated so much that the flight controller was constantly overcorrecting. I tuned the correction down, and the drone nosedived and shattered the first time I pushed it forward. That showed me the frame material was the real problem, not the tuning. I rebuilt it on carbon fiber and the vibration went away.

Occupancy detection research

STEM@GTRI · Georgia Tech Research Institute

How do you tell if a room has people in it, using only sensors? My question was whether combining several sensor types detects people better than any single one. I tested four approaches: environmental sensing (temperature, humidity, CO2, light), microphones, PIR motion sensors, and mmWave radar. Each picks up a different kind of evidence that someone is there.

Method

I reflashed M5Stack educational boards to stream environmental, audio, light, PIR, and mmWave data, and recorded all of them in the same room so the results could be compared directly.

Findings

Microphones and light sensors reacted to people, but also to things that weren't people, so they produced false positives. Running all of them side by side showed each sensor's strengths and weak spots, and in combination they largely covered for each other: where one sensor missed or misfired, another usually got it right. PIR detected presence more reliably than mmWave, but that says more about the hardware than the method: the mmWave module I had was unreliable. I presented the results at STEM@GTRI and recommended testing a better mmWave radar, since a good one can detect people who are sitting still, which PIR can't.

Error Humanizer

Offline debugging tool

Developers in research labs and government settings often can't use AI tools, so they debug by hand. The Error Humanizer helps with that. You pick a language, paste an error log, and it explains the error in plain English with the usual fixes.

How it works

Each language has its own set of regular expressions that pull the error type, message, and stack trace out of the log. The tool matches the error type against a JSON file of explanations and common fixes for that language. It runs entirely offline, which is what makes it usable where AI tools aren't allowed. It supports eight languages, including COBOL, which still runs a lot of government and banking systems. Adding a language means adding one pattern set and one JSON file.

The Gainesville Guide

Automated local events newsletter · Part-time software developer role

A local entrepreneur introduced me to AI agent tools and brought me on as a part-time software developer to build a weekly newsletter covering things to do in Gainesville, Georgia. I designed it to run mostly on a pipeline of AI agents, and I act as the editor who signs off on every issue before it goes out.

How it works

Scrapers pull events from local sources, including RSS feeds, into a SQLite database. A drafting agent queries that database and writes each issue in a defined brand voice. Before anything is published, every event has to pass source-verification rules, and the draft waits for my approval. It ships two editions: a Wednesday list covering the full week and a Thursday weekend guide grouped by day.

What broke

Some sources, like city and chamber sites, looked promising and returned nothing usable, so the pipeline runs on the feeds that actually work. Recurring runs also couldn't be scheduled reliably against the local database, so each issue still needed me to start it by hand.

Where it stands

The first three issues, published in September 2026, were a proof of concept: the pipeline could find events, write an issue, and get it out the door. I've paused regular publishing while I improve the pipeline on my end, starting with scheduling and better sources, so it can run every week without me starting it.

Electric Vehicle

Science Olympiad build event · 5th place, regional

The event is a precision problem: drive a small car a set distance in a straight line and stop as close to the target as possible. Mine runs on an Arduino Nano, with an IMU for heading and a start button to launch the run.

Design decisions

From the wheel diameter I know how far the car travels per wheel rotation, so for any target distance I can work out how long the motors need to run. To keep the car straight, the Nano reads the IMU's heading and uses a PID loop to correct the steering as it drives.

What broke

At first the PID loop didn't correct hard enough, so the car still drifted off its line. Raising the proportional gain fixed most of it. Once tuned, the car held a straight line and finished about 6 inches from the target.

Next year

Stopping on time assumes the motors always spin at the same speed, but they slow down as the battery drains. I'm adding wheel encoders so the car counts actual wheel rotations and stops on distance, not time.

ESP32 display builds

Two small builds, made as gifts

Two small ESP32 builds, each in an enclosure I designed and 3D printed.

Photo frame

Cycles through JPEGs stored on an SD card and shows them on a 1.3-inch ST7789 screen. Decoding the images meant working with a JPEG library that hands you each picture in small blocks through callbacks, so I had to manage where each block gets drawn and how memory is handed back and forth. That is what finally made pointers click for me.

The display and the SD card share the same SPI bus, and SD cards are bad citizens on a shared bus: they don't always let go of it cleanly when another device needs it. The card kept failing, so I had the firmware reset it in software each time, which got it reading reliably.

Day counter

A battery-powered counter that keeps time with a DS3231 real-time clock and shows the day count on a 16×2 character LCD. The count has to roll over at local midnight, so I had to handle time zones properly instead of trusting the clock's raw time.
